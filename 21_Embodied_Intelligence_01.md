## 1. Embodied intelligence and large language models

First, a statement: this is only a basic academic discussion. It does not involve specific company names or products. Please do not post bad comments.

First of all, I really dislike the name "embodied intelligence." We already have robots. Why create a new term? It is also hard to type with an input method. A robot is already defined as a machine close to human form.

I need to briefly explain how a large language model, or LLM, works. It is basically a text-processing program. It takes text in and produces text out. Whether it runs on a CPU or GPU does not change its nature. It has no three-dimensional feeling. More importantly, its calculation time is uncertain. It cannot respond to the real world in real time like humans. More precisely, response delay and response cycle are different things.

A large model does not really exist in the world like a human. It looks at pictures from the last few seconds and then decides. A faster decision cycle reduces its intelligence, so its actions are somewhat jerky. If movement is smooth, we can basically assume it was programmed in advance.

The actual process is more complex. First comes image recognition. The image must be turned into text: understand its content and say it. The model then decides by talking to itself, and issues commands by talking too. This is literally giving an order, such as moving xxx forward five centimeters.

When a human presses a button, they can judge and immediately react to how far it is pressed, whether it has been touched, and whether it feels right. Human body decisions do not go through a text channel.

## 2. The body's distributed decisions

More precisely, early animals with nervous systems, such as jellyfish, had nerves closer to a system combining negative and positive feedback. There was no consciousness; consciousness appeared completely later. Each nerve ending has sensory signals. It decides how strong a signal to send based on the strength it senses in itself. Other neurons compare incoming signals with their own and decide whether to move forward or backward. The whole body then moves.

This is a highly distributed decision system. Sensors and movement organs are almost directly connected, with only a little communication with other nerves. Physical reality largely allows this. So the first step is really to build sensors, make sure they can connect directly to movement organs, and implement some functions. This does not depend on any large language model.

For example, build a four-legged robot without an upper body. Feed in different sensor signals and let the computer directly command the motors, without a vision model. It is like turning in circles on the ground with your eyes closed without falling. This is only the first step, but is already very hard.

Current sensors depend on vision. A major problem is that there are almost no other sensor types. Foot-pressure sensors and "xxx" sensors may be needed, followed by acceleration and tilt sensors.

## 3. A control structure based on the brain

Next, decision-making. We can actually copy the human brain model. It can be understood as three layers: spinal cord, cerebellum, and cerebrum. The spinal cord is the sensor layer just discussed and keeps the whole body stable. The cerebellum controls specific movement adjustments. I have not yet seen a robot with a cerebellum part.

Then comes the cerebrum, which makes decisions. Another special part is the striatum, which can approve movement. It is like a valve or voting body in the brain. Think of the brain as having several thoughts, with the strongest controlling behavior. There must be only one action, because there is only one body. It can go forward or backward, with no middle state. This is a quantum property.

Many decision plans are good, but mixing them and taking their average gives the worst result. Think of a truck on railway tracks moving forward or backward.

The brain also has a prefrontal cortex, like a grand secretary of the cabinet. It makes higher-level decisions or analyzes incoming signals in higher dimensions. This part can actually be outside the robot, with cloud analysis, and may not need constant use. In other words, the robot does not really need a brain as long as it can work. "xxx." Removing the brain can reduce manufacturing costs.

## 4. Action limits and vision processing

One special feature of the cerebellum is that it is a collection of actions. This can limit robot behavior, such as forbidding attacks on humans. To attack a human, a robot must clearly recognize that it wants to attack, send the attack command to the cerebellum, and let the cerebellum carry out the movement.

We only need to remove the related paths in the cerebellum beforehand. It also performs simple checks on whether decisions are reasonable. The cerebrum wants to use the "attack" skill, but the cerebellum does not offer it. Of course, a very smart robot may find design loopholes, such as deliberately breaking lights. That becomes very difficult.

Visual nerves can also follow two routes, like in humans. One goes to decision-making, while the other goes to lower-level systems for body balance.

## 5. Robot shapes and cooperation in buildings

Here are some engineering suggestions. First, give up walking on two legs and use a wheeled base. This works better with lifts and is more stable indoors. Second, allow more weight and a higher carrying capacity to reduce computation costs.

There must be several cameras. The two main cameras must be far apart to form stereo vision. The large difference between their views can make up for algorithm limits. Try closing one eye and making your two index fingers touch accurately. In theory, this is impossible.

Two or three robot hands are enough. They do not need to copy humans with exactly two symmetrical hands. The three hands can differ: one supports and lifts things, one does precise work, and a longer one reaches distant objects without moving the body.

Another key point is that computer systems connect well and can interact efficiently with many machines. Cameras in a building should share more information with robots. For example, a robot going upstairs can carry items other robots need transported, then leave them somewhere without a face-to-face handover. A building may even need cupboards that only robots can open. Hallway cameras can also let robots see themselves and judge their position and posture.

The building also needs central management to coordinate each robot's temporary working area. It judges the flow of people, has robots avoid blocking routes, and settles conflicts between robots. They can move a little faster in safe places and more slowly elsewhere.

There may also be special lifts for robots, smaller and faster with lower safety requirements. In addition to stairs, buildings may need gentle ramps that robots can use easily.

More importantly, robots do not need to look the same. Only their working components need to stay consistent. More temporary models can be assembled. A building with narrow corridors can get smaller robots. A building with only stairs and no ramps may need walking robots. These temporary models are just presets among many models, not completely unique designs. Different robots can also cooperate.
