## 1. Limits of the current technical approach

Let us get more specific. First, this technical approach is already badly outdated. I remember people doing it in 2019. There were many "AI moving boxes" videos. GPUs trained many "AI life forms" with several moving joints in a computer world. This existed long ago. Robots can now be built perhaps mainly because vision-model problems have been solved. Electric motors have also become stronger and more precise.

But I still do not think this is the right approach. A robot cannot simply be defined as many rigid bodies and joints. The parameters of GPU training models are too simple. A rigid body's relationship with other rigid bodies cannot be described by one point. The body itself cannot be simulated as one point either. Its center of mass and geometric center may differ, and it is not an ideal geometric shape.

In simple terms, simulation accuracy needs to improve. In practice it cannot, because even a small improvement increases computing costs by orders of magnitude. There is no especially good solution now.

## 2. Training and real-world adjustments

Joint design should follow human bones. Human bones have many kinds of contact, including hinges and ball joints. Reducing a robot's flexibility can also reduce training costs.

Adjustments should still happen after manufacturing. First, train roughly on a computer to make sure basic limb angles and movement directions are right. A human wearing sensors can demonstrate this with their body. Then fine-tune the robot in reality. But this only ensures some basic tasks. All movements are really trained in advance. This is the biggest bottleneck now, and I cannot think of a way past it.

Training and actual use are completely separate. Robots cannot learn in daily life. More computing power cannot break through this either. The physical or mathematical structure is incomplete and lacks necessary parts. I do not know exactly what is missing.

## 3. Possible product directions

If I must suggest something, first give up walking on two legs. Keep only one robotic arm, fine-tune it, make highly accurate simulations, and keep improving a limited set of actions until it is the best in its field. Such products may also find buyers in industry.

I hope there will be a buyback system, or that the robots can only be rented out. This reduces the burden on factories. I also hope prices can be lower. Robots would then perform more consistently in fixed factory tasks.
