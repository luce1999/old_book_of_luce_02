## 1. Co-op mode

First, co-op mode. Many RTS games balance around 1v1, but I do not want co-op to have a separate extra stat table. It can add some technologies, upgrades, and talents while using the competitive mode's balance data, fixed to a certain version. It may catch up with the competitive version later, but not necessarily.

Computer players will behave as much as possible like human 1v1 players. There will not be many races; they will mostly match 1v1. Players can choose talents, all unlocked by default. There may also be special units or neutral buildings where mercenaries can be bought.

A major problem with SC2 co-op is "cooperation for its own sake." Objectives force two players to stand on points at the same time, which is troublesome. We should divide roles strategically: each player defends a direction, one handles air units and the other ground units, or one defends while the other attacks. This will not be forced. Roles can follow terrain and spawn points, or players can decide based on races and talents.

For player count, I think three is better. Solo co-op should belong to the campaign. SC2's two-player co-op mainly makes the game online; with only one player, people may not buy paid heroes. Two players can also argue easily. With three, even if one is less skilled, the other two can control the situation. Four makes coordination difficult and limits map design more. Three offers the most map choices. For example, two players can share a base while the third has an outside base, or bases can connect through different routes. There are many options.

One terrain style should not have only one map. Reuse assets as much as possible and make more "isomers." Destructible elements such as "xxx" can also be reused to add randomness. A map can have two or more enemy races: a main enemy and a more static, defensive faction. Extra objectives can also have some randomness.

## 2. Enemy AI and mission design

One key issue is that computer behavior must not be too obvious. It must not always attack in wave after wave, like stronger tower defense. Players should judge when to expand to another resource site. The computer must also judge attack routes. It can go around a strong defense or even choose not to attack. It can also develop its economy. Overall, it should think more like a human player and make flexible decisions. I have designed part of a decision tree. This can be a separate topic, and designing it will also be allowed in the future.

Co-op objectives should work both ways. If humans took the place of the enemy race, they should also have a game experience. In other words, even perfectly correct decisions should not guarantee a 100% win rate.

There is also a special co-op mode, closer to a tutorial. It requires a party and is more like a multiplayer campaign. One player has less content and mainly supports the other, making it easier for experienced and new players to play together. Overall, the game should stay interesting, with something new each time. Players can also start directly with computer teammates if they dislike human teammates, and basic commands will be available.

## 3. Campaign and target players

The campaign mainly teaches. The first missions introduce the game and its units. Middle missions become harder, with more complex scenes and gameplay closer to 1v1, but limited units, unusual terrain, and objectives to complete. Later missions can add special gameplay. Larger missions can feature layered defenses, gradual fighting and retreating, and similar scenes.

I am mainly making this game for the most dedicated RTS players. I will not give much consideration to casual passers-by.

The source code can be read, but it cannot be recompiled for commercial use. There are no rights beyond reading it. Almost all the code is generated by large language models, or generated from keywords under my supervision. Early on, I will aim for good enough. Later, every line will be checked by hand, perhaps with community help. Once interfaces are fixed, I will try not to change them and will keep forward compatibility.

A lot has been made, and it basically runs. But progress has reached a bottleneck and needs more of my effort. Large language models are still limited, and further adjustment is needed.

Game balance can rely partly on large models. There are roughly two kinds of AI: classic RTS AI such as decision trees, and large models. Large models cost more. They need multidimensional data modeling, then fine-tuning based on the results. This can be discussed separately.

## 4. Platform and mod design

For a larger game with some mod support, I suggest not making the game first and the editor later. Build a general gaming platform first, then present the main game content as a mod.

For third-party content, do not provide only a mod interface. At least include database options for changing basic data or adding unit types. Basic skill interfaces would be good too. Do not restrict xxx in mods. Allow hooks directly or provide suitable entry points.

Mod types and their allowed scope must be strictly defined, such as UI, unit types, terrain, and equipment. Everything uses registration interfaces. Direct changes to existing content are not allowed. Mod conflicts are serious. Mods must declare dependencies, and the game checks whether different mods change the same content. This step is fairly simple. Load order also matters. Basic content loads first, and later content may override it. Distinguish mod names properly to avoid naming conflicts.

The official developer must provide enough components or plugins, both as examples and to test the engine's abilities. Common functions should also have macros prepared for direct use.

For the language, use Lua 5.1 directly. It is the most general approach. Do not design bytecode or similar things; just use Lua. But restrict its functions yourself. Do not invent your own programming language.
